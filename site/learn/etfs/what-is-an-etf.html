What is an ETF?

One purchase, hundreds of stocks. How exchange-traded funds work, why they stay honest, and what to check before you buy one.

The basket idea

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of investments you buy in a single trade. One ticker can hold hundreds of stocks — buy one share of a broad-market ETF and you own a tiny slice of every company inside it. It trades on an exchange all day, just like a stock: live prices, a bid and an ask, and market and limit orders all work exactly the same. If you can buy a stock through your brokerage, you can buy an ETF.

Key terms

TermPlain-English meaning
ETFA fund that trades like a stock.
NAV (net asset value)What the basket's holdings are actually worth per share, updated through the day.
Expense ratioThe fund's annual fee, as a percent of your money. 0.10% means about $10 a year on $10,000.
Authorized participantA large trading firm allowed to swap baskets of stocks for ETF shares (and back). The behind-the-scenes crew that keeps the price honest — more below.
Tracking errorHow far the ETF's returns drift from whatever it follows. Small is good.

How the price stays honest

Here's the clever part. Think of an ETF like a vending machine selling variety packs. When demand pushes the pack's price above what the contents are worth, a restocker buys up the individual items, hands the full set to the machine, and receives new packs to sell — pocketing the difference. When the pack trades too cheap, the restocker runs it in reverse: buys packs, breaks them into items, sells the items. That constant restocking — called creation and redemption — is why an ETF's market price hugs the value of what's inside it. You never see it happen; it runs behind the scenes all day, and the authorized participants do it for the small profit, not out of kindness.

Why people use them

  • Instant diversification in one trade. A single broad-market ETF can spread you across hundreds of companies — no need to pick and manage each one.
  • Low cost. Many broad ETFs charge a fraction of what old-school mutual funds did.
  • Trades anytime the market is open. Unlike a mutual fund, which prices once a day after the close, you can buy or sell an ETF at 10:04 AM at a live price.
  • No minimum beyond the share price. One share is enough to start.

What they don't do

  • Not every ETF is a calm, diversified basket. Some track a single theme, a single country, or a single commodity — and leveraged ETFs reset their exposure daily, which can grind returns down in choppy markets. The label says how it's packaged, not how risky it is.
  • The price can briefly wander from NAV in fast markets.
  • You still carry market risk. A diversified basket falls when the whole market falls — diversification spreads risk around, it doesn't remove it.

The cost math

A quick illustration of why the expense ratio deserves your attention. Put $10,000 in an ETF charging 0.10% a year: about $10 a year in fees. Put the same $10,000 in a fund charging 1.00%: $100 a year. Same market exposure, ten times the fee drag — and fees compound against you year after year, so the gap grows the longer you hold.

Key point. Before buying any ETF, check three things: what's inside the basket, what it costs per year, and whether it trades with a tight bid/ask spread. Those three answer most of the questions that matter.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming "ETF" means safe. The wrapper isn't the risk — the contents are. A leveraged single-theme ETF can be wilder than any individual stock.
  • Ignoring the expense ratio. It's small print that compounds for decades.
  • Buying a thinly traded ETF. Wide bid/ask spreads are a hidden cost on every single trade. Check the spread before you buy, not after.
  • Expecting mutual-fund behavior. ETFs trade live and can be bought on margin and optioned; mutual funds price once daily. Same basket idea, different machinery.

The takeaway

An ETF is a basket you buy like a stock, kept honest by professional restockers arbitraging any gap between the pack price and its contents. For most beginners, a broad, cheap, heavily traded ETF is the simplest diversification they'll ever buy. Next up: index ETFs — the default answer for most people, and why.

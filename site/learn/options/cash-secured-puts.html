Cash-secured puts: getting paid to wait for a stock you want

Sell a put, collect the premium, and either keep it or buy the stock at a discount you chose. The put side of the income coin.

What it is

A cash-secured put is the mirror image of the covered call. Instead of owning shares and selling a call against them, you keep cash on the sideline and sell a put. You collect the premium up front. In exchange, you agree to buy 100 shares at the strike price if the stock falls there. Two endings, both acceptable: the stock stays up and you keep the premium, or it drops and you buy a stock you wanted at a price you picked in advance — minus the premium you already collected.

Key point. You must actually want the stock. A cash-secured put obligates you to buy 100 shares at the strike — only sell puts at strikes you'd happily buy at.

Key terms

TermPlain-English meaning
Selling a putYou sell someone the right to sell you shares at the strike. You collect cash now; you hold the obligation.
Cash-securedYou keep the full cost of 100 shares in cash, ready if you're assigned. A $50 strike means $5,000 set aside.
StrikeThe price you'd buy at if assigned.
PremiumWhat you collect for taking on the obligation. Quoted per share; one contract covers 100 shares.
AssignmentThe put buyer exercises their right and you buy 100 shares at the strike — usually automatic at expiration if the stock is below it.

Worked example

An illustration with round numbers. Say a stock trades at $52 and you'd be happy owning it at $50:

  • Sell one $50 put, about a month out, for $1.50 → collect $150 (1 × 100 × $1.50). Set aside $5,000 in cash.

Two endings:

  • The stock stays above $50 through expiration. The put expires worthless. You keep the $150, your $5,000 is free again, and you can sell another put next month. This is the most common outcome — and the whole point.
  • The stock drops and you're assigned. You buy 100 shares at $50 ($5,000). But you already collected $150, so your effective cost is $48.50 per share. You wanted the stock at $50; you got it at $48.50.

When it beats a limit order

A limit order to buy at $50 just sits there waiting. A cash-secured put at the $50 strike pays you to wait for the same fill. If the stock never drops, the limit order gets you nothing; the put seller still banks the premium. The trade-off: a limit order can be cancelled any time, while a short put commits you until expiration (unless you buy it back). Use the put when you'd genuinely buy at the strike; use the limit order when you want a free option to change your mind.

The honest risks

  • The stock can keep falling past your strike. You agreed to buy at $50; if it goes to $40, you're down roughly $1,000 per contract minus the premium. A cash-secured put is still a bullish trade — you have to want the stock.
  • Your cash is tied up. The $5,000 securing the put can't do anything else while the trade is on.
  • Early assignment happens. Less common on the put side, but a buyer can exercise before expiration, especially around dividends and corporate actions.
  • Selling puts on stocks you don't want is speculation, not income. If assignment would feel like a punishment, the trade was wrong from the start.

What you need from a broker

Selling puts requires an options approval level that covers it — brokers gate this because the obligation is real. You also need the cash to secure every contract you sell: one contract at a $50 strike ties up $5,000, two contracts tie up $10,000, and so on. Common mistakes

  • Selling puts without the cash to back them. That's a naked put — a different, far riskier trade with margin requirements that can surprise you.
  • Selling more contracts than your cash covers. Count the full strike × 100 × contracts before you sell, not after.
  • Chasing the fattest premium on a shaky company. High premium means the market sees high risk. A 10% monthly yield on a put usually means the stock might actually go there.
  • Forgetting earnings. A stock can gap straight through your strike overnight on an earnings report. Check the calendar before selling puts into one.
  • Selling puts on a stock you wouldn't buy. Then assignment day feels like a loss instead of the plan working.

The takeaway

A cash-secured put is a limit order that pays you. Pick a stock you'd buy anyway, pick a strike you'd buy at, keep the cash aside, collect the premium. Assigned? You own the stock below the strike. Not assigned? The premium was the whole point. And when you do get assigned, the natural next move is selling a covered call against those new shares — the two trades together are called the wheel, and that's the next lesson.

Dividends explained: getting paid to hold

What dividends are, how ex-dates work, and why a 12% yield is usually a warning, not a gift.

What a dividend is

Some companies share their profits with shareholders as cash payments called dividends — usually paid quarterly, quoted per share. Own 100 shares of a company paying $0.50 a quarter and you collect $50 every three months, $200 a year, just for holding. Not every company pays one: many growth companies reinvest every dollar back into the business instead. Neither choice is automatically better — it's a different way of returning value.

Key terms

TermPlain-English meaning
Dividend yieldAnnual dividend ÷ stock price. A $2 yearly dividend on a $50 stock = 4% yield.
Ex-dividend date (ex-date)The cutoff. Buy before it and you get the dividend; buy on or after it and you don't.
Record dateThe day the company checks its books to see who gets paid — usually right after the ex-date.
Payout ratioDividends ÷ earnings. Above 100% means the company pays out more than it earns — not sustainable for long.

The ex-date: no free money

Here's the part beginners miss: on the ex-date, the exchange reduces the stock price by roughly the dividend amount. A $50 stock paying a $0.50 dividend opens around $49.50 that morning. The dividend isn't free money — you get the cash, but your shares are worth correspondingly less at the open. Buying the day before the ex-date just to "capture" the dividend is a wash before taxes, and after taxes it's usually a small loss.

Yield math

Yield is just a ratio, and ratios move when either side moves. A $2 annual dividend on a $50 stock reads as 4%. If the stock drops to $25 and the dividend holds, the screen now flashes 8% — the same dollars, a very different story. A rising yield often means a falling price, not a rising payout. Always ask which side moved.

Key point. A 12% yield is usually a warning, not a gift. Prices collapse when the business is in trouble — and troubled businesses cut dividends. Check the payout ratio and whether earnings actually cover the dividend before trusting a fat yield.

Yield traps

An illustration of the trap. Imagine a stock that fell from $100 to $40 while still paying $4.80 a year. Screeners light up: "12% yield!" But the price fell for a reason — earnings are sliding, and the payout ratio is climbing past what the business can support. Then the company halves the dividend to survive, the "12%" was never real, and you're holding a stock down 60% paying half of what you expected. High yield + falling price + rising payout ratio is the classic warning pattern. A sustainable 3–4% from a healthy company beats a flashing 12% from a troubled one almost every time.

Dividends and options

One practical overlap with this site's income strategies: if you sell covered calls on a dividend-paying stock, the call buyer can exercise early to capture the dividend — taking your shares before expiration. Always check the ex-date before selling calls on dividend payers; an early assignment the day before the ex-date is the market working exactly as designed.

Common mistakes

  • Chasing the highest yield on the screener. See "yield traps" above. Sort by yield and you're sorting by distress.
  • Forgetting the ex-date price drop. Dividend capture sounds clever until the opening print erases it.
  • Assuming dividends are guaranteed. They're voted every quarter and can be cut or suspended. Nothing about a dividend is a promise.
  • Ignoring the stock while counting the cash. A 4% dividend doesn't help much on a stock down 30%. Total return — price change plus dividends — is the only score that matters.

The takeaway

Dividends are real cash for holding, and a long record of steady or growing payouts says something genuine about a business. But always ask why the yield is what it is. Price, payout ratio, and earnings coverage tell you whether you're looking at income — or a trap dressed up as one.

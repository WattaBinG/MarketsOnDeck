September 15, 2026Market Tape

The Market Tape — End-of-Day Wrap

SPY closed today at $757.86, down 0.40% from Monday's $760.65 — a second straight loss, and now roughly 2.6% below the $777.88 closing high the index set August 13. But today's decline had almost nothing to do with the AI-safety story that drove Monday's session; it was a different set of mechanics entirely.

The real driver was energy. WTI crude jumped 3.7% to $105.12 a barrel and Brent rose 2.4% to $108.20, after Saudi Aramco reportedly canceled or delayed cargoes to European refiners, per Benzinga's midday wrap. U.S. diesel prices climbed to a record average of $6.26 a gallon, above the previous high set in 2022, with heating oil futures up 5.5% and gasoline up 3.2%. President Trump wrote on Truth Social that "the world's diesel price rise is mostly caused by the Russia/Ukraine War, not Iran." It showed up unevenly across the three benchmarks: DIA, the most industrial-heavy of the three, fell 0.60% to $521.36, a shade worse than QQQ's 0.56% drop to $705.18 and SPY's 0.40% decline, with the Energy sector the only one of the eleven S&P sector SPDRs posting a real gain on the day, per Benzinga.

Layered on top of the oil move, the 10-year Treasury yield extended its climb for a fifth straight session, touching 5.04% intraday before settling near 5.00%. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent addressed it directly with lawmakers, saying "the need to address [the] deficit is one factor affecting 10-year treasury bond yield," while stressing Treasury doesn't "set equilibrium price for yield levels." That backdrop sits directly ahead of tomorrow's Fed decision: per Benzinga's citation of the CME Group's FedWatch tool, traders are now pricing roughly a 92% probability of a 25-basis-point hike at Wednesday's 2 p.m. ET announcement — which would be the Fed's first hike since July 2023, and is now less than a day away.

That yield move is a real, mechanical headwind for one of my own positions. SoFi (SOFI), 100 shares in the Trading account (account ending ••••8840) at a $17.48 average cost, closed down 3.12% to $17.10 — for the same balance-sheet-lender reason this brief has flagged before: higher long-term rates raise SoFi's funding costs and widen securitization spreads on a loan book it carries directly, not because of anything company-specific today. The position is now down about 2.2% from cost.

The other real story of the day happened in Washington, not on a trading desk: the Senate's procedural cloture vote on the CLARITY Act, the crypto market-structure bill, failed to reach the 60 votes needed to advance, per CNBC, after last-minute Democratic objections to the bill's ethics and stablecoin-yield provisions. Coinbase (COIN), which isn't a position I hold, closed down 10.25% to $171.83 — roughly four times bitcoin's own decline — while Circle Internet Group fell nearly 9% intraday on the same news, per Benzinga. Bitcoin itself closed down 2.56% to $75,674.57, ether fell 3.96% to $2,399.43, and solana dropped 4.57% to $96.90: a real pullback, but a much smaller one than what hit the exchanges and legislation-linked equities most directly exposed to today's vote.

The weekend's AI-safety debate kept threading through my other two accounts today, this time from a new angle. At the All-In Summit, Elon Musk warned that existing chip fabs are running at full capacity and that a disruption in Taiwan could compound the shortage — the reason, he said, Tesla and SpaceX are building their own "Terafab" facility. SpaceX (SPCX), 150 shares in the Trading account at a $123.71 average cost, closed down 3.25% to $143.33, still up about 15.9% on cost; the Agentic account's SPCH, a fund targeting twice SPCX's daily move, fell 6.61% to $9.19, still up roughly 12.6% on its $8.16 average cost. KLA Corporation (KLAC), the Agentic account's other position (2.28 shares at a $189.93 average cost), was named in the same Terafab coverage as one of the equipment suppliers that could benefit if fab-building shifts outside Taiwan — it closed down just 0.39% to $168.43, a quiet day after Monday's 6% drop on the Third Point disclosure, still down about 11.3% from cost. Quantum Computing (QUBT), 500 shares in the Trading account at an $8.81 average cost, fell 1.90% to $7.82 with no company-specific news I could confirm today — it reads as broad small-cap weakness (the Russell 2000 fell about 0.9%, per Benzinga) rather than its own story, now down about 11.2% from cost.

Why this matters: three separate mechanisms moved money today — an oil-supply shock, a multi-year high in long-term rates, and a specific legislative setback for crypto market-structure rules — and none of them needed an AI headline to do it. Worth sitting with after a week where the AI-safety debate has dominated the narrative: real markets keep running more than one story at once, and a position like SoFi can have a genuinely bad day for reasons that have nothing to do with whatever's trending that week.

The Market Tape — End-of-Day Wrap

SPY closed today at $760.65, down 0.48% from Friday's $764.35 — a second straight loss that picked up almost exactly where this morning's brief left off, on the same weekend AI-safety story that hit futures before the open.

The session turned into a real capital rotation, not just a broad risk-off day. AI-infrastructure hardware kept bleeding: Nvidia fell 3.57% to $210.50, AMD dropped 4.45% to $493.17, and Intel slid 5.59% to $97.19. Chip-equipment names took it further — Applied Materials and Lam Research each fell roughly 6% and ASML sank about 5%, per 24/7 Wall St.'s Monday coverage of what it called the "AI pacing call reaching fab spending." Hewlett Packard Enterprise dropped 9% (on a downgrade after a big recent run, a company-specific reason layered on top of the sector weakness), with Dell off 5% and Super Micro down 8% in the same AI-server complex. Cybersecurity names jumped hardest of all: CrowdStrike and Zscaler each rose roughly 12% and Palo Alto Networks about 11%, after Microsoft published a new "Humanist AI Code of Conduct" framework that investors read, per Benzinga's reporting, as a reason to own the security layer sitting on top of increasingly autonomous AI systems.

One data point today made the weekend debate feel less like market mood and more like an actual operating decision: per Yahoo Finance, Nvidia and Palantir — along with Booz Allen Hamilton — are reportedly restricting their own use of advanced models from Anthropic and OpenAI over concern those models could retain or learn from sensitive corporate data, and are building an alternative using Palantir's software paired with Nvidia's own open Nemotron models instead. Anthropic's response, per the same report, is a new "Enterprise Frontier Safeguards" framework that stores customer activity data in infrastructure the customer controls. That's a real company decision tied to this weekend's essay, not just a repricing of sentiment.

Why this matters: Bank of America's own read today, per Benzinga, was that Amodei's AI-safety warning is "noise" in what the firm still calls a "secular bull market" — worth holding next to today's very real, very large sector-level moves. A capital rotation out of AI-infrastructure hardware and into software and cybersecurity isn't the same thing as a broad market selloff, and today's crypto and Dow-adjacent action both back that reading up. Where it gets less academic is a position like KLAC: a stock can get hit by a real sector story and a real, if lagging, ownership-change disclosure on the very same day, and it's worth being able to tell those two forces apart rather than reading one closing number as a single cause.

Worth being precise about what that does and doesn't mean: on a day the broad market is rallying on a friendly macro print, a name can absorb a negative analyst call and still close green just by riding the tide — that's a different, weaker kind of "up" than a stock rallying on its own news, and today's green close isn't itself evidence that BMO's thesis is wrong.

(This wrap draws on the Trading account only, not the Agentic account, so it isn't a full-book summary.)

One thing is now a few trading days closer: the Fed's rate decision lands Wednesday, September 16, following the FOMC's two-day meeting that starts Tuesday. Today's in-line inflation read is one of the last major data points the committee sees before that decision — it removes one argument for a hawkish surprise without, on its own, confirming anything about what comes next.

Why this matters: a rally built on an in-line inflation print is a "no bad news" rally, not a "good news" one — worth keeping that distinction in mind before reading too much bullish signal into today's green close. And a stock that rallies through a bearish analyst note on a day the whole market is up isn't the same as that stock proving the note wrong — two things worth keeping separate heading into next week's Fed decision, not blurred into "the market liked LULU today."

September 11, 2026Morning Brief

Drafted premarket, before the 8:30 a.m. ET CPI release.

SPY, the S&P 500 ETF, closed Thursday at $757.83, down 0.60% from Wednesday's $762.40 and now about 2.6% below the $777.88 closing high it set August 13 — ground that looked reachable two weeks ago is slipping further away as the week winds down.

The number the market's been building toward all week lands at 8:30 a.m. ET this morning: the Bureau of Labor Statistics' August Consumer Price Index, the last inflation read the Federal Reserve gets before it meets September 15–16. Economists surveyed ahead of the release are looking for headline CPI to ease slightly to a 3.3% annual rate from July's 3.4%, on a 0.4% monthly gain, with core CPI (excluding food and energy) expected at 0.2% month-over-month and 2.4% year-over-year.

Thursday's Producer Price Index offered an early, imperfect preview, and it didn't settle much. Headline PPI rose 0.4% for the month, in line with expectations, but the annual rate came in at 5.4% — a tenth hotter than forecast and a hot reading by its own recent standards. (Worth noting PPI isn't the Fed's actual inflation gauge — the 2% target is defined on PCE, not producer prices — so this is a read on pipeline cost pressure, not a direct measure against the Fed's own goalpost.) Core PPI undershot on the month, at 0.2% against a 0.3% forecast. Read together, that's producer-level cost pressure still running hot annually even as the monthly core trend cooled slightly — not a print that points cleanly in either direction.

What's actually raised the stakes on today's number is last week's jobs report. Nonfarm payrolls jumped 162,000 in August against a consensus estimate of just 53,000, June and July were revised up a combined 55,000, and unemployment held at 4.1%. That reversal from months of soft prints is a real part of why the market has shifted from pricing in a rate cut at next week's meeting toward actually weighing the odds of a hike — a repricing that showed up directly in Thursday's trading.

The clearest place it showed up was semiconductors. Nvidia fell 2.26% to $218.36 and Intel dropped 5.57% to $100.32 Thursday, both landing among the most actively traded names in the entire market — a sharp reversal from the sector-wide rally this brief covered on September 8. High-multiple, rate-sensitive growth names are typically first to give ground when the market recalibrates toward a tighter-for-longer path, and chips are carrying some of the highest multiples in the index right now.

That same rate-sensitivity thread runs directly through a name I actually hold in my Trading account: SoFi Technologies, down 0.69% to $17.21 Thursday. SoFi operates as a digital bank carrying real loan volume — personal, student, and mortgage originations — directly on its own balance sheet, so a higher-for-longer rate path raises its funding costs and pressures the fair-value marks on that existing book, not just sentiment around the stock. It's a smaller, quieter move than NVDA's or INTC's, but arguably a more direct read on what a hawkish surprise this morning would actually cost this particular position.

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX) is the other position worth flagging today, for an unrelated reason — this one's company-specific, not macro. CFO Bret Johnsen told Goldman Sachs' Communacopia + Technology Conference Thursday that SpaceX closed another compute-hosting deal worth roughly $1.11 billion a month starting in December, adding more than $13 billion in annualized revenue toward the company's own public target of $100 billion in ARR by year-end.

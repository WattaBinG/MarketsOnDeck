September 4, 2026Morning Brief

Originally drafted September 4, 2026 at approximately 6:27 a.m. ET, premarket — before that session's outcome was known. Added to this public archive on September 9, 2026; the wording below has not been edited using subsequent market results.

SPY, the S&P 500 ETF, closed Thursday at $773.17, up from $765.16 on Tuesday and sitting about 0.6% below the $777.88 closing high set August 13 — close to record territory, not quite there.

The name actually moving the premarket tape this morning is one I hold: Lululemon (LULU), down roughly 19.4% in premarket trading as of about 6:25 AM ET, from Thursday's $121.77 close to about $98.20, after the company reported fiscal Q2 results after Thursday's close and cut its full-year guidance. That's meaningfully below my $99.93 average cost on 55 shares.

The headline EPS number was actually a beat against the reduced expectations the market had settled into after the year's earlier guidance cuts — $2.92 reported. The problem is underneath it: revenue fell 4% year over year to $2.4 billion, and comparable sales fell 9% on a reported basis (10% on a constant-dollar basis). North America revenue fell 8% (U.S. down 8%, Canada down 11%), and on the earnings call management pointed specifically to "negative commentary across media and social channels" weighing on store and digital traffic in both North America and China.

The guidance cut is what's doing the real damage. Lululemon now expects fiscal Q3 EPS of $0.93 to $0.98 and full-year EPS of $9.48 to $9.73 — down from the $10.95 to $11.15 range it guided to last quarter.

Before reacting to a move like that, the question worth asking is whether it's company-specific or an early read on consumer discretionary broadly. Checking premarket action in Nike (roughly flat), TJX (roughly flat), Deckers (roughly flat), and Abercrombie (roughly flat) points to the former — none of those four are trading in sympathy, which suggests a Lululemon-specific traffic and brand-sentiment problem rather than a sector-wide rotation. That's four names, not a full sector scan, so it's a lean, not a conclusion.

Why this matters: an EPS beat doesn't protect a stock when guidance and comp sales are both moving the wrong direction — the headline number only tells part of the story. And since the peers I checked didn't move with LULU this morning, this reads as a name to actually dig into the earnings call on, not a signal to reposition around anything broader. I'm not adding to or trimming the position this morning; what I'd want to see before deciding either way is whether the North America traffic trend management described actually stabilizes next quarter.

September 8, 2026Morning Brief

SPY closed Friday at $770.19, about 1% below the $777.88 close it put in on August 13 — a level the index has been circling for most of the past month without a clean break higher. The index itself only slipped 0.39% on the day, but that headline number was hiding the real story sitting one layer down, in semiconductors. Today's open comes after the Labor Day holiday closed markets Monday, so there's one extra day of news since Friday's close to work through, on top of the usual weekend.

SOXL, the leveraged semiconductor ETF, jumped 10.12% Friday while its inverse, SOXS, fell almost exactly the same amount — a clean tell that this was directional flow into the group, not noise. What made Friday different from a typical NVDA-only pop is that Broadcom and AMD firmed alongside Intel on the same tape, and the financial press covering it that day was framing Nvidia less as a single chip vendor and more as the infrastructure layer underneath the broader AI buildout — with Intel, Broadcom, and AMD described as riding the same supply-constrained demand rather than competing for a fixed slice of it.

That's a different shape of move than one company re-rating on its own numbers — it reads more like a sector getting bid as a group on an infrastructure thesis. Tesla was the counterpoint on the same tape, down 5.92% to $354.08, a reminder that "risk-on" wasn't a blanket theme Friday; the strength was specific to the AI/semis complex, not growth stocks broadly.

Two names in my own book also moved today. Lululemon (LULU) closed at $103.19, up 2.56% — and the timing isn't incidental: today, September 8, is Heidi O'Neill's first day as CEO after joining from Nike. I added 55 shares Friday at an average cost of $99.93 during last week's guidance-cut selloff, so today's move puts the position meaningfully in the green rather than just breakeven. Worth being honest about the causality, though: a first-day pop on a new CEO is a common, well-known pattern, not evidence the turnaround itself is real — that gets proven in results over the next few quarters, not in how the stock trades on day one.

SPCX also had a real day — closing at $153.47, up 3.73% on roughly 85.8 million shares, with an intraday high of $155. I hold 150 shares at an average cost of $123.71, so this is a position that's been working for a while (up about 24% on that cost basis), and today's move extends it rather than starting anything new. I don't have a confirmed single catalyst for today's specific pop beyond broader momentum in the name.

Why this matters: the semis move (SOXL/SOXS/INTC/AVGO/AMD/NVDA) is the one with real market-structure significance — it's a sector repricing together, not one name. LULU and SPCX are personal-position updates, not signals about the broader market: one is a new-CEO date lining up with a bounce, the other is an existing winner extending its run. I don't hold any of the semiconductor names, so nothing there is riding on my own book — but between the sector move and today's two personal updates, this is a fuller day than the SPY's quiet 0.39% headline number would suggest on its own.

SPY closed Tuesday at $765.96, down 0.55% from Friday's $770.19 close (Monday was Labor Day, markets closed) — a quiet number on its own. What's actually setting up today's tape is oil: Brent crude cleared $100 a barrel for the first time in about six weeks early this morning, up from Tuesday's $97.89 settle, after another wave of Middle East attacks — Iran-backed Houthi forces claiming responsibility for hitting Saudi Arabia's Jazan refinery, and U.S. strikes on Iranian oil tankers after talks over passage through the Strait of Hormuz broke down. Both Brent and U.S. crude are up more than 60% for the year. Morgan Stanley's read this morning, via a note picked up in Alpha Vantage's news feed, is that the wars in Iran and Ukraine together are pushing oil traders out of long-term positions entirely and into shorter-dated, more selective derivatives — a market bracing for more volatility, not pricing in a resolution.

Futures this morning are down modestly across the board on that — Dow futures off roughly 0.3%, S&P 500 futures down about 0.2%, Nasdaq-100 futures down around 0.4%. Chip names are doing more than their share of that damage: NVDA slipped about 0.6% premarket, enough to drag the VanEck Semiconductor ETF down close to 0.9%. That's notable because NVDA was already the session's laggard Tuesday, closing down 2.01% to $225.73 even as the rest of chips ran the other way.

INTC led that divergence Tuesday, up 9.05% to $104.47 on nearly 140 million shares — one of its more active days all year, and on a stack of catalysts rather than one clean story. Northland Securities upgraded the stock to Outperform with a $120 price target, citing turnaround progress and continued tight supply of server processors. That landed alongside a supply-chain report that Intel plans to raise PC processor prices roughly 10% starting in early October — the third such hike this year, after increases in Q1 and again in July. Intel Foundry and ASML also disclosed they've now processed more than a million wafers using High-NA EUV lithography, a real manufacturing milestone for the foundry business Intel is trying to stand up as a genuine third leg. Worth distinguishing that stack of operational news from Tuesday's other headline on the name — a Trump social media post touting the company — which isn't operational news at all.

That chip strength wasn't confined to Intel, either. KLA Corporation (KLAC), a chipmaking-equipment maker I hold a small position in through my Agentic account (account ending ••••1117, 2.28 shares at a $189.93 average cost), closed Tuesday up 1.82% to $188.98 — gaining alongside the same foundry-capacity buildout that's lifting Intel, including the same High-NA EUV supply chain ASML is part of. It's a small position, but it's one more data point that Tuesday's chip move was a sector story, not just an Intel one.

The position in my own book that actually moved on new information this morning is Lululemon (LULU), 55 shares at a $99.93 average cost in my Trading account, added during last week's guidance-cut selloff. LULU closed Tuesday at $103.19, still carrying some of the bounce from Heidi O'Neill's first day as CEO. But two analysts trimmed price targets on the stock within minutes of each other early this morning: Citigroup cut to $117 from $130, Daiwa Securities cut to $105 from $120, both keeping Neutral ratings. Per FactSet, the Street's average rating on LULU is still just "hold," with a mean price target of $99.67 — sitting almost exactly on my own cost basis rather than clearly above or below it. That's a useful tell: the new-CEO optimism that moved the stock Tuesday hasn't shown up in analysts actually raising numbers yet — if anything, two firms went the other direction the same morning. That reads as a reason to keep watching the position, not as validation of anything.

SPCX, the other position moving today, is doing so for less company-specific reasons — shares were lower in premarket trading alongside the broader market slide tied to oil, with no SpaceX-specific headline behind it that I could confirm. I hold 150 shares in my Trading account at a $123.71 average cost, a position that's been working for a while, so a macro-driven red premarket doesn't change anything about it on its own. My Agentic account also holds a small position in SPCH, a 2x leveraged daily SpaceX ETF, which by design is simply tracking roughly double SPCX's move today — not a separate story, just amplified exposure to the same one.

Two real dates worth having on the radar: the Producer Price Index prints before Thursday's open, and the Consumer Price Index follows Friday morning, with consensus looking for headline CPI around 3.4% year-over-year and core CPI closer to 2.4%. The Fed is also already in its pre-meeting quiet period ahead of the September 15-16 FOMC decision, so any market-moving Fed commentary this week isn't going to come from the Fed itself.

Why this matters: today's real story is oil, not any single stock — a $100 Brent print on live Middle East escalation is the kind of macro shock that can override whatever a company's own fundamentals are doing, and it's worth knowing that's the backdrop before reading too much into any one name's move today, including the positions in my own book — across both accounts — that are actually in play: LULU and SPCX in the Trading account, KLAC and SPCH in the Agentic account. INTC and KLAC's moves are a legitimate sector story worth understanding on their own terms; LULU's is a reminder that a stock price and the analyst numbers behind it don't always move together, and that gap is worth watching rather than acting on. Nothing here changes what I'm doing with any position this morning.

SPY closed Wednesday at $762.40, down 0.46% from Tuesday's $765.96 and now about 2% below the $777.88 closing high it set on August 13 — its second straight lower close after Tuesday's own retreat from Friday's $770.19. Nothing in that headline number looks dramatic on its own, but the real action Wednesday was one layer down, in a name I actually hold outright.

SpaceX (SPCX) fell 3.86% to $147.55 on 119.9 million shares — the second-most actively traded stock in the entire market that session, per Alpha Vantage's most-active list. The move wasn't news-driven in the usual sense: per Benzinga's reporting, it tracked the release of roughly 319 million previously locked-up shares becoming eligible for sale, the latest stage of SpaceX's staggered post-IPO lockup schedule. That's the same mechanism, and almost the same magnitude, as an August 20 unlock that also knocked the stock down about 4% before it recovered the very next session. A mechanical share-supply event like this reads differently than a fundamentals-driven selloff — the float grows, some early holders take profit, and the stock tends to find its level again once the initial supply gets absorbed.

I hold 150 shares in the Trading account at an average cost of $123.71, so even after Wednesday's drop the position is still up about 19% on that cost basis — a pullback inside a winning trade, not a thesis-threatening one. The smaller Agentic account carries a version of the same exposure: a small position in SPCH, a fund that targets twice SPCX's daily move, also up meaningfully from its own average cost and also down alongside SPCX on Wednesday, for the same mechanical reason rather than any news specific to the fund. Premarket Thursday has SPCX essentially flat around $147.56, but there's a reason not to read too much into that calm: per the same Benzinga reporting, a further 59.1 million affiliate-held shares reach their own unlock eligibility date today, with another roughly 328 million following on September 24. This isn't a one-time event — it's a staggered supply release that's likely to keep showing up on the tape through the rest of the month.

Zooming out, today is a real inflection point for the broader market regardless of any single name: the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the August Producer Price Index at 8:30 a.m. ET this morning, with August CPI following tomorrow, Friday, September 11, at the same time. Back-to-back inflation prints landing a day apart tend to move rate-cut expectations more than either report would on its own — the kind of macro backdrop that can add noise to individual stock moves, including one like SPCX's, that actually have nothing to do with inflation at all.

Why this matters: it's worth being able to tell the difference between a stock falling because something changed about the business and a stock falling because more shares became sellable — SPCX's Wednesday move (and SPCH's, right alongside it) was the latter, and today's flat premarket action is already leaning toward confirming that read. Separately, with PPI due this morning and CPI tomorrow, this is the kind of stretch where broader market swings are worth watching for what they say about rate-cut odds, not for what any single stock is doing.

SPY, the S&P 500 ETF, closed Thursday at $758.04, down 0.57% — down from Wednesday's $762.40 and now about 2.6% below the $777.88 high it set on August 13. It was the fourth straight losing session for the major averages, and the story underneath today's tape was less about any single company and more about what's happening in the energy market.

WTI crude settled at $102.48 a barrel, up 6.7% on the day, with Brent finishing at $107.63, up 5.9% — both benchmarks back above $100 for the first time in months. The move tracks reporting of intensified attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz following a reported U.S. strike on Iranian oil vessels earlier in the week. I'm not in a position to verify the underlying military reporting beyond what's been published by wire services, and it's genuinely still developing — but the market reaction to it is the relevant part here: a sudden jump in energy costs is itself an inflation input, and it landed on a day that already had inflation data on the calendar.

That data was this morning's Producer Price Index for August, released at 8:30am ET. The headline monthly number (+0.4%) actually matched what was expected. What moved markets more was the trend underneath it: the 12-month unadjusted rate came in at 5.4%, up from July's 4.8% pace, and the core reading (excluding food, energy, and trade services) still rose 0.3% for the month and 4.7% over the past year. A monthly print that's in line can still read as a warning sign if the year-over-year trend is accelerating — and that's what happened today, on the same session oil spiked.

One name in my own book moved on its own story rather than the broader tape: Lululemon (LULU) closed at $96.55, down 3.18%, and slipped back below my $99.28 average cost on the 100 shares I hold in my Trading account — giving back Tuesday's post-CEO-announcement bounce to $103.19, and then some. The average cost itself moved this week too, worth logging plainly rather than just showing the new aggregate: I bought 45 more shares Wednesday morning at 9:40am ET, filled at $98.49, on top of the 55 shares held since last week's guidance-cut selloff — that trade, not any restatement, is what moved the average from $99.93 to $99.28. Two real, dated items explain today's move better than the broad tape does: BMO Capital Markets initiated coverage on LULU Wednesday with an "underperform" rating and a $70 price target, and Levi & Korsinsky put out a notice the same day that it's investigating LULU's disclosures related to IEEPA tariff refunds. I can't independently verify either beyond the fact that both were published — an analyst initiation and a plaintiff-firm investigation notice are two different kinds of signal, and the second one in particular is worth treating as exactly what it is (a law firm soliciting a case) rather than confirmation of wrongdoing. Either way, it's a more plausible explanation for today's move in that specific name than anything in the broader tape.

The rest of the Trading account mostly just moved with the market — SoFi (SOFI) down 0.69% to $17.21, Quantum Computing (QUBT) down 2.74% to $7.82. SPCX had a wide session, swinging from $144.89 to $154.70 before closing at $147.81, up 0.17% on the day; I don't have a confirmed catalyst for that range beyond ordinary volatility in a name that already trades with a lot of it. (This wrap pulls positions from the Trading account only, same as it's scoped to do — the Agentic account isn't part of this read.)

One more thing now a day closer: August's CPI is due out tomorrow morning, Friday, September 11, at 8:30am ET. Today's PPI trend and the oil move both raise the stakes on that number a little — a hot CPI print on top of today's action would be a different market than a cool one.

Why this matters: today wasn't really a stock-picking day — it was an energy-and-inflation day, and the LULU move is a reminder that even on a macro-driven tape, individual positions can still have their own reasons for moving that have nothing to do with the index. Worth remembering the difference between "my position is down because the market's down" and "my position is down for its own reason" before reacting to either one.

September 11, 2026Morning Brief

This entry contains affiliate links and reflects my own opinions and positions. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell any security, and past results don't predict future ones. Drafted premarket, before the 8:30 a.m. ET CPI release.

SPY, the S&P 500 ETF, closed Thursday at $757.83, down 0.60% from Wednesday's $762.40 and now about 2.6% below the $777.88 closing high it set August 13 — ground that looked reachable two weeks ago is slipping further away as the week winds down.

The number the market's been building toward all week lands at 8:30 a.m. ET this morning: the Bureau of Labor Statistics' August Consumer Price Index, the last inflation read the Federal Reserve gets before it meets September 15–16. Economists surveyed ahead of the release are looking for headline CPI to ease slightly to a 3.3% annual rate from July's 3.4%, on a 0.4% monthly gain, with core CPI (excluding food and energy) expected at 0.2% month-over-month and 2.4% year-over-year.

Thursday's Producer Price Index offered an early, imperfect preview, and it didn't settle much. Headline PPI rose 0.4% for the month, in line with expectations, but the annual rate came in at 5.4% — a tenth hotter than forecast and a hot reading by its own recent standards. (Worth noting PPI isn't the Fed's actual inflation gauge — the 2% target is defined on PCE, not producer prices — so this is a read on pipeline cost pressure, not a direct measure against the Fed's own goalpost.) Core PPI undershot on the month, at 0.2% against a 0.3% forecast. Read together, that's producer-level cost pressure still running hot annually even as the monthly core trend cooled slightly — not a print that points cleanly in either direction.

What's actually raised the stakes on today's number is last week's jobs report. Nonfarm payrolls jumped 162,000 in August against a consensus estimate of just 53,000, June and July were revised up a combined 55,000, and unemployment held at 4.1%. That reversal from months of soft prints is a real part of why the market has shifted from pricing in a rate cut at next week's meeting toward actually weighing the odds of a hike — a repricing that showed up directly in Thursday's trading.

The clearest place it showed up was semiconductors. Nvidia fell 2.26% to $218.36 and Intel dropped 5.57% to $100.32 Thursday, both landing among the most actively traded names in the entire market — a sharp reversal from the sector-wide rally this brief covered on September 8. High-multiple, rate-sensitive growth names are typically first to give ground when the market recalibrates toward a tighter-for-longer path, and chips are carrying some of the highest multiples in the index right now.

That same rate-sensitivity thread runs directly through a name I actually hold in my Trading account: SoFi Technologies, down 0.69% to $17.21 Thursday. SoFi operates as a digital bank carrying real loan volume — personal, student, and mortgage originations — directly on its own balance sheet, so a higher-for-longer rate path raises its funding costs and pressures the fair-value marks on that existing book, not just sentiment around the stock. It's a smaller, quieter move than NVDA's or INTC's, but arguably a more direct read on what a hawkish surprise this morning would actually cost this particular position.

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX) is the other position worth flagging today, for an unrelated reason — this one's company-specific, not macro. CFO Bret Johnsen told Goldman Sachs' Communacopia + Technology Conference Thursday that SpaceX closed another compute-hosting deal worth roughly $1.11 billion a month starting in December, adding more than $13 billion in annualized revenue toward the company's own public target of $100 billion in ARR by year-end. The stock (held in the Trading account) was among the most actively traded names in the market Thursday (116 million shares) and closed up 0.43% to $148.18. Conference remarks aren't the same as an actual filed number, so this is a data point to watch for confirmation on, not a settled fact — but it's a real one on a position that's already up meaningfully from my $123.71 average cost.

Why this matters: today's CPI print is the last inflation read the Fed gets before deciding, next Wednesday, whether the rate path the market has been assuming for months still holds. A hotter-than-expected number would likely push the "higher-for-longer" repricing further into names like the chip stocks that already wobbled Thursday — and quietly, into rate-sensitive names like SoFi that don't show up on any top-movers list but carry real balance-sheet exposure to exactly this outcome. Worth watching how the market actually trades the print this morning, not just where the headline lands against consensus.

That data point was August's CPI report, which held at 3.4% year-over-year, unchanged from July and in line with what the market had already priced in. The reaction was immediate: by 10:38 a.m. ET, the Dow was up more than 1%, the Nasdaq up 1.20%, and the S&P up 1.08% on the in-line print, with oil trading lower and gold catching a bid — the shape of a fairly standard "no surprise, no reason to reprice" relief rally. That's about as bearish a note as a stock gets in a single morning, and the tape mostly shrugged it off. Worth being precise about what that does and doesn't mean: on a day the broad market is rallying on a friendly macro print, a name can absorb a negative analyst call and still close green just by riding the tide — that's a different, weaker kind of "up" than a stock rallying on its own news, and today's green close isn't itself evidence that BMO's thesis is wrong.

The rest of my Trading-account positions — SOFI, QUBT, SPCX — moved with the broad market today; I didn't find anything ticker-specific attached to any of the three, which fits a session where one macro print was doing the work rather than individual stock news. (This wrap draws on the Trading account only, not the Agentic account, so it isn't a full-book summary.)

One thing is now a few trading days closer: the Fed's rate decision lands Wednesday, September 16, following the FOMC's two-day meeting that starts Tuesday. Today's in-line inflation read is one of the last major data points the committee sees before that decision — it removes one argument for a hawkish surprise without, on its own, confirming anything about what comes next.

Why this matters: a rally built on an in-line inflation print is a "no bad news" rally, not a "good news" one — worth keeping that distinction in mind before reading too much bullish signal into today's green close. And a stock that rallies through a bearish analyst note on a day the whole market is up isn't the same as that stock proving the note wrong — two things worth keeping separate heading into next week's Fed decision, not blurred into "the market liked LULU today."

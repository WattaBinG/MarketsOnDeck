## Before You Start: What You're Actually Deciding

Opening a brokerage account is easy. Deciding what to buy is the part that actually matters, and it's not something a walkthrough article can responsibly do for you — anyone telling you exactly what to buy as your first trade, with no idea of your situation, risk tolerance, or goals, is giving you advice they're not qualified to give. This guide covers the mechanical steps: how an account gets set up, how an order actually works, and what happens after you click submit. What you buy is your call, ideally after your own research.

## Step 1: Create Your Account

1. Download the Robinhood app or go to robinhood.com and sign up with your email.
2. You'll be asked for basic personal information — legal name, address, date of birth, and Social Security number. This is required by federal law for any brokerage account (it's how they verify identity and report to the IRS), not a Robinhood-specific overreach.
3. Answer a few questions about your employment and investing experience — this affects what account features (like margin or options) you're initially eligible for, and it's normal to start at the most restricted tier.

## Step 2: Verify Your Identity

Robinhood will typically ask for a photo of a government ID and sometimes a selfie for verification. This usually takes minutes, occasionally up to a day if manual review is needed. This step exists across every regulated U.S. brokerage — it's not unique friction Robinhood adds.

## Step 3: Link and Fund Your Account

1. Connect a bank account — Robinhood supports linking via Plaid (instant) or manual routing/account number entry (takes a few business days to verify).
2. Choose how much to deposit. **Start small.** There's no rule that says your first deposit needs to be substantial — plenty of people fund with $50-100 just to learn the mechanics of the app before committing more.
3. Instant deposits (usually up to a few hundred dollars, depending on your account) are typically available immediately; larger deposits may take a few business days to fully settle.

## Step 4: Placing Your First Order

This is the actual mechanics — how an order works, using a widely-held index fund as the example purely to demonstrate the process, not as a recommendation:

1. Search for a ticker in the app (for this walkthrough, we'll reference **VOO**, an S&P 500 index fund, purely because it's a common, well-understood example — not a suggestion that it's right for you).
2. Tap **Trade**, then **Buy**.
3. Choose your order type:
   - **Market order** — buys immediately at the current price. Simple, but you don't control the exact execution price.
   - **Limit order** — you set the maximum price you're willing to pay; the order only fills if the stock reaches that price or better. More control, no guarantee it fills at all.
4. Enter how many shares (or, on Robinhood, a dollar amount if you want fractional shares) you want to buy.
5. Review the order summary — confirm the order type, quantity, and estimated cost — then submit.
6. You'll get a confirmation once the order fills. Market orders during trading hours typically fill within seconds; limit orders may take longer or not fill at all if the price never reaches your limit.

## Step 5: After the Order Fills

Your position now shows up in your portfolio, tracking in real time against the current market price. A few things worth understanding before you place a second order:

- **Unrealized gain/loss** is just the current paper value — it isn't real until you actually sell.
- **Settlement time** matters if you plan to use sale proceeds quickly — stock trades typically settle in one business day (T+1) in the U.S. as of 2024's shortened settlement cycle, meaning funds from a sale aren't immediately available to withdraw or reinvest without restriction in some account types.
- Robinhood, like other brokerages, shows your order history and tax documents (1099s) — keep an eye on this for tax season regardless of how small your first trade was.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a lot of money to start?**
No. Robinhood supports fractional shares, meaning you can buy a portion of an expensive stock for as little as $1. Starting small to learn the mechanics is a completely reasonable approach.

**Is my money safe if Robinhood has problems?**
Robinhood is a member of SIPC, which protects securities and cash in your account up to $500,000 (including $250,000 for cash) if the brokerage itself fails — this doesn't protect against market losses, only against the brokerage's own failure.

**What's the difference between a market order and a limit order, really?**
A market order prioritizes speed — you'll definitely get filled, but not at a guaranteed price. A limit order prioritizes price — you control the maximum you'll pay, but there's no guarantee it fills at all if the stock never reaches your limit.

**Should my first trade be an individual stock or a fund?**
This is a real decision with tradeoffs (concentration risk vs. diversification, research burden, volatility) that depends on your own goals and risk tolerance — genuinely outside what a walkthrough article should tell you. If you're unsure, that uncertainty itself is worth sitting with before funding an account, not rushing past.
